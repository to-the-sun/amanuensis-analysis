# Mathematical Simplification of Additive Synthesis Equations

This document explores whether it is possible to reduce or simplify the equations used in additive synthesis (such as those generated by `additive_synthesis.py`), and discusses the mathematical and practical trade-offs of various notations.

---

## 1. The Short Answer: Irreducibility of Distinct Frequencies
Under normal circumstances, the long addition chain of sinusoids is **literally the simplest explicit time-domain equation** that exactly describes the sound.

In signal processing and linear algebra, sinusoidal functions of distinct frequencies are **orthogonal**. That is:
$$\int_{0}^{T} \sin(2\pi f_1 t) \sin(2\pi f_2 t) \, dt = 0 \quad \text{for } f_1 \neq f_2$$

Because they are orthogonal, they represent independent "dimensions" or components of the signal. There is **no trigonometric identity** that can collapse two sines of different frequencies into a single simpler sinusoid or reduce the number of terms. If your sound has 8 distinct harmonic frequencies, the mathematical expression *must* have at least 8 distinct sinusoidal terms to be represented exactly in the time domain.

---

## 2. Compact Representations (How We Can "Simplify")
While we cannot reduce the underlying mathematical complexity of distinct frequency sines, we can represent them in more compact notations.

### Option A: Sigma ($\sum$) Notation
The most common way to simplify a long, repetitive chain of terms on paper is by using Sigma summation notation. Instead of writing:
$$p(t) = A_1 \sin(2\pi f_1 t + \phi_1) + A_2 \sin(2\pi f_2 t + \phi_2) + \dots + A_K \sin(2\pi f_K t + \phi_K)$$

We can write:
$$p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{K} A_n(t) \sin(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n)$$

Where:
- $K$ is the number of active components.
- $A_n(t)$ represents the time-varying amplitude envelope of the $n$-th partial (for example, the exponential decay $A_n e^{-d_n t}$ in the `"juicy"` bell preset).
- $f_n$ is the frequency of the $n$-th partial.
- $\phi_n$ is the phase offset.

### Option B: Piecewise / Closed-Form Equations (Infinite Limit)
For certain idealized periodic wave shapes, summing an **infinite** number of terms yields a series that can be rewritten as a simple, non-trigonometric periodic function in the time domain:

* **Infinite Square Wave**:
  $$p(t) = \text{sgn}(\sin(2\pi f_0 t))$$
* **Infinite Sawtooth Wave**:
  $$p(t) = 2 \left( f_0 t - \lfloor f_0 t + 0.5 \rfloor \right)$$

**The Limitation:** This simplification only works for an infinite number of terms. The moment we truncate the series to a finite number of steps $K$ (which is exactly what `additive_synthesis.py` visualizes to show how a wave is constructed), we cannot use the simple closed-form formula anymore. The truncated sum produces ripples (the Gibbs phenomenon), and describing those ripples exactly *requires* writing out the individual sines.

### Option C: Euler's Formula & Complex Exponentials
In engineering and advanced signal processing, equations are often simplified by moving into the complex domain using **Euler's Formula** ($e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$). This allows us to represent sines and cosines as the real part of complex exponentials:

$$p(t) = \text{Re} \left( \sum_{n=1}^{K} \tilde{X}_n e^{i 2\pi f_n t} \right)$$

Where $\tilde{X}_n = A_n e^{i\phi_n}$ is a single complex number (a **phasor**) that encodes both amplitude and phase. While this looks extremely clean and simplifies algebraic manipulations (such as integration, differentiation, and filtering), it still represents $K$ separate terms under the hood.

### Option D: Detuned Beating (Sum-to-Product Identities)
In synthesizer design, we often detune oscillators slightly to create a thick, moving "chorus" or "unison" effect (seen in the reverb tail components of the `"juicy"` preset). When two sinusoids of equal amplitude have very close frequencies ($f_1$ and $f_2$), we can simplify their sum into a single term using the **sum-to-product identity**:

$$\sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t) = 2 \cos\left(2\pi \frac{f_1 - f_2}{2} t\right) \sin\left(2\pi \frac{f_1 + f_2}{2} t\right)$$

This simplifies two added terms into a single sine wave of the average frequency $\frac{f_1 + f_2}{2}$ whose amplitude is slowly modulated (multiplied) by a low-frequency cosine envelope.

## 3. Implementation in `additive_synthesis.py`
To keep the visual display clean and digestible while visualizing high step counts, the visualizer uses **Sigma ($\sum$) notation** to show the compact representation at each step, while plotting and playing the exact accumulated audio.
